Orbit Privacy Policy

Effective date: 30 August 2026
Last updated: 30 August 2026

Orbit is an app that helps students find other students to study with at the same time, on the same thing.

This policy explains what we collect, why, who can see it, and how you get rid of it. It's written to be read, not skimmed past. If something here isn't clear, email us and ask.

Orbit is not affiliated with, endorsed by, or connected to the University of British Columbia. We are an independent app. UBC has no involvement in Orbit and no access to your Orbit data.

Orbit is operated by an independent developer based in British Columbia, Canada. Orbit is not incorporated and is not a company.

We are subject to British Columbia's Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA) and, where it applies, Canada's Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA).

3. Why we collect it

We use your information for these purposes and no others:

  • To run matching. Your courses, availability grid, year, faculty, and preferences are compared against other users' to find people studying the same subject at the same time.
  • To show you a profile and show your profile to others. See section 4 for exactly who sees what.
  • To let you message and plan sessions with people you match with.
  • To keep Orbit safe. Blocks, reports, and reviewing reported content.
  • To send you account emails, such as confirming your email address or resetting your password. Orbit does not currently send push notifications and does not collect a push token.
  • To operate and fix the app, including debugging problems.

We do not sell your personal information. We do not share it with advertisers. There are no ads and no payments in Orbit.

4. What other users can see — read this section

This is the section that matters most. Be honest with yourself about what you're comfortable making visible before you fill in your profile.

The information other students can see

If someone can see your profile, they can see:

  • Your display name
  • Your profile photo (if you added one)
  • Your year of study
  • Your major
  • Your pronouns
  • Your gender — unless you selected "prefer not to say", in which case nothing is shown
  • Your study style
  • The courses you're taking
  • Your whole weekly availability grid — every block you marked free. Read the next part before you fill it in.

Your faculty, intensity, session length, preferred environments and study goals are used to rank and filter matches, but are not displayed on your profile.

Your availability grid reveals when you are and are not free

This deserves saying plainly, because it's the least obvious thing on this page.

Anyone who can see your profile sees your entire weekly availability grid — all 35 blocks, exactly as you filled it in. Not just the times you overlap with them. All of it.

From that, a stranger can infer a good deal about your week: when you're in class, when you work, which evenings you're out, whether you're around on weekends. Marked free every weekday at 9am and never on Friday nights? That is now visible to every person you match with, and you cannot see who looked.

So fill it in as an invitation, not as a diary. Mark the blocks you actually want someone to contact you about, and leave the rest unmarked — including blocks where you're free but would rather not be asked. Unmarked doesn't mean "busy", it just means "don't ask", and nobody can tell the difference.

Who can see your profile

Someone sees your profile only if all of these are true:

  1. Your account is set to discoverable. If you turn discoverability off, you stop appearing in matching.
  2. You are taking at least one course in common. Orbit's matches list is built around studying the same thing, so people with no shared course don't appear on it at all.
  3. You both pass each other's match filters. If they don't fit your preferences for year, faculty, gender or group size, they don't see you — and the reverse is also true. Both directions are enforced, so setting a filter narrows who can see you as well as who you see.
  4. Neither of you has blocked the other, and neither has passed on the other. A block removes visibility in both directions.
  5. Neither account is banned.

Availability is not a gate. Someone whose free blocks overlap yours is shown higher up the list, but people who haven't filled in their grid still appear.

What other users never see

  • Your email address
  • Your gender, if you selected "prefer not to say" — that choice is honoured and no gender is shown
  • Your deadlines
  • Your blocks — the person you blocked is not told
  • Your reports — the person you reported is not told who reported them

One honest caveat about match preferences. The filters you set — which years, faculties or genders you want to be matched with — are not shown anywhere in the app. But they are stored on your profile row, and the app's data rules currently allow another signed-in user to retrieve them directly if they go looking, rather than through the app's screens. Nothing in Orbit shows them, and an ordinary user will never see them, but we would rather tell you than let you assume a guarantee we haven't built yet. We're closing this.

Photos and images are stored at public web addresses

Please read this before you add a profile photo or send a picture in a chat.

Your profile photo, and any image you send in a course chat or a direct message, is uploaded as a file to our storage provider and given a plain web address. The profile and the message that shows the image are protected by the access rules above — but the image file's own web address is not. Anyone who has that address can open the image in a browser without signing in to Orbit.

In practice, the address is only handed out to people who can already see the profile or the message. But if one of them copies the link and passes it on, or posts it somewhere, the image is viewable by whoever they gave it to, and it stays viewable.

What this means for you: treat any image you put into Orbit as something that could end up outside Orbit. Don't send a picture through Orbit that you would not be comfortable with a stranger seeing.

We consider this a shortcoming rather than a design choice, and we intend to move image files behind expiring, permission-checked links. When we do, we'll update this section and say so.

Once you send it, you can't take it back

Anything you type into a message or a profile field can be screenshotted, copied, or remembered by the person who sees it. Deleting it from Orbit does not delete it from someone else's phone. Don't put anything in your profile or a message that you'd be upset to see shared.

5. Consent, and how to withdraw it

By creating an Orbit account and filling in your profile, you consent to us collecting and using your information for the purposes in section 3.

You can withdraw consent at any time:

  • Turn off discoverability. You stop appearing in other people's matching. Your account stays.
  • Change or clear the optional parts of your profile. You can set your gender to "prefer not to say", clear your study and match preferences, empty your availability grid, and delete any deadline, at any time.
  • Delete your account. This removes your information from Orbit. See section 10.

Being straight with you about two limits in the current version of the app:

  • You can change your profile photo, but there is no button to remove it entirely. If you've added one and want it gone, email the privacy address in section 1 and we'll delete the file for you. We're adding a remove option.
  • Your name, year, faculty, major, study style, and pronouns can be edited but not left blank. The app won't save a profile with any of them empty. If you don't want to state a major or pronouns, both fields accept a general answer, and pronouns include "prefer not to say".

Withdrawing consent for required information means you can't keep using Orbit — matching doesn't work without courses and availability. That's the trade-off, and it's a legitimate one, but you should know it upfront.

Withdrawal isn't retroactive. It doesn't undo things that already happened, like a message someone already read.

6. Messages

Messages between users are stored in our database so they can be delivered and so you can read your history.

Messages are not end-to-end encrypted. They are encrypted in transit and at rest by our hosting provider, but they are not encrypted in a way that stops us from reading them. That means:

  • If a message is reported, we may read it — the reported message and enough surrounding conversation to understand what happened.
  • We do not read messages for any other routine reason. We don't read them out of curiosity, we don't scan them for marketing, and there is no advertising to target.
  • We may also access messages if we're legally required to — for example, a valid court order — or if we genuinely believe someone is at risk of serious harm.

If you need a private, encrypted conversation, use a messaging app built for that. Orbit is built for coordinating study sessions.

7. Automated filtering and moderation

The automatic filter

Orbit checks the text you type against a list of slurs and explicit sexual terms before it is sent. This applies to messages, your display name, and your pronouns. The check ignores common letter-substitution tricks, so writing a blocked word with numbers or symbols in place of letters does not get around it.

  • The check runs on your own device, as you send. Nothing is sent to a third party to be scanned, and no AI classifier is involved.
  • If text matches, it is refused and never sent — you're shown a message and can edit it. We do not store the refused text, and a refusal on its own is not reported to us or held against your account.
  • The filter only reads text. It does not scan images. Images are reviewed only when someone reports them.
  • The filter is deliberately narrow. It blocks slurs and explicit sexual content — it does not police rudeness, disagreement, or bad jokes. Those are handled by reporting and blocking.

No automatic filter catches everything, and this one is not meant to. It is a first line, and reporting is the real one.

Human moderation

Moderation at Orbit works like this today:

  • You report. Reports come from users. Every report goes into a queue that a human — currently the founder — reviews.
  • We act within 24 hours. We commit to reviewing and acting on reports of objectionable content and abusive users within 24 hours of receiving the report.
  • Acting can mean removing content, hiding a profile, suspending an account, or permanently banning a user.
  • Blocking is instant and automatic. When you block someone, visibility is cut in both directions immediately. No review needed, no explanation needed.

Decisions are made by a person, not an algorithm. If you think a moderation decision about your account was wrong, email the support address in section 1 and a human will look at it again.

Orbit's database, authentication records, and stored files are hosted by Supabase on servers located in the United States.

That means your personal information leaves Canada and is stored on computers in another country.

What this means for you: while your information is in the United States, it is subject to United States law. US courts, law enforcement, and government agencies can, in certain circumstances, compel access to information held there — including under US national-security and surveillance laws — without notifying you. Canadian privacy law cannot prevent that.

We think the practical risk to a study-matching profile is low, but you're entitled to know before you sign up, and BC privacy law requires us to tell you.

How to ask about it: if you have questions or concerns about where your information is held, contact our Privacy Officer at the email in section 1.

10. Retention and deletion

While your account is active, we keep your information so the app works.

When you delete your account, we delete:

  • Your profile and every field in it
  • Your profile photo file, and every image you sent in a chat, from storage
  • Your messages, in both course chats and direct messages
  • Your matches
  • Your study sessions and invitations
  • Your deadlines, study preferences, match preferences, and availability grid
  • Your authentication record — the login credential itself, so the account is genuinely gone, not just hidden

Deletion is not reversible. We can't restore an account after you delete it.

What may survive deletion, and why:

  • Anything the other person already saved themselves. Your messages are removed from our database, so the person you were talking to opens the conversation and finds it empty — your messages are gone from their app, not just hidden. What we cannot undo is a screenshot, a copied link, or a saved image already on someone else's phone.
  • Safety records. If you were reported or banned, we keep a minimal record — an internal identifier and the reason — so a banned user can't immediately return with a new account. Reports filed about you are kept but stripped of the link to your identity. We keep this for 12 months and no longer than needed for that purpose.
  • Records we're legally required to keep, for as long as the law requires.
  • Backups. Our database provider keeps encrypted backups so the service can be restored after a failure. Deleted information can remain in those backups until they roll over, currently up to 7 days, after which it is gone. Backups are never used to bring a deleted account back.

11. How to delete your account

In the app: open Settings, scroll to Account, and tap Delete my account. You'll be asked to confirm. Once you confirm, deletion happens immediately and as described in section 10. You do not have to email us, and you do not have to leave the app.

If the in-app option isn't working, email the privacy address in section 1 from the email address on your account and ask us to delete it. We'll do it and confirm within 30 business days, usually much faster.

13. Security

What we do:

  • Your data is encrypted in transit (HTTPS/TLS) and encrypted at rest by our hosting provider.
  • Access to the database is controlled by row-level security rules — the rules that enforce who can see whose profile are enforced by the database itself, not just by the app on your phone.
  • Passwords are handled by Supabase Auth and stored hashed, never in plain text. Nobody at Orbit can see your password.
  • Access to the production database is limited to one person — the developer, who is also the Privacy Officer — through an account protected by multi-factor authentication. Nobody else has credentials. If that ever changes, we'll update this policy.
  • Exception: image files. As explained in section 4, profile photos and images sent in chats are stored at public web addresses and are not protected by the rules above.

What we can't promise: no system is perfectly secure. We can't guarantee your information will never be accessed by someone who shouldn't have it. What we can promise is that we'll take it seriously and tell you if it happens — see section 15.

Your part: use a strong, unique password, don't share your login, and don't put sensitive personal information (financial details, health information, your home address) into your profile or messages. Orbit has no reason to ask for any of that.

14. Age restrictions

You must be 17 or older to use Orbit.

We set the floor at 17 because some first-year university students are still 17 when term starts, and excluding them would cut out part of the group Orbit is built for.

This means some people on Orbit are under 19, which is the age of majority in British Columbia. If you are an adult using Orbit, assume you may be matched with, or messaged by, someone who is a minor. Behave accordingly. If you are under 19, everything in our safety guidelines applies to you with more force, not less — read them before you agree to meet anyone.

We do not verify anyone's age. We ask you to confirm you're 17 or older at sign-up and we take your word for it. Anyone can type anything into that box.

We don't knowingly collect personal information from anyone under 17. If we find out an account belongs to someone under 17, we'll delete it and its data.

If you believe someone under 17 has an Orbit account, or that an adult is behaving inappropriately toward a minor, email the privacy address in section 1. Reports involving a minor's safety go to the front of the queue.

15. If there's a data breach

If your personal information is lost, stolen, or accessed by someone who shouldn't have it, and there's a real risk of significant harm to you, we will:

  1. Tell you as soon as feasible — what happened, what information was involved, what we're doing, and what you should do.
  2. Report it to the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada, as PIPEDA requires, and notify the OIPC BC where required.
  3. Keep a record of the breach, as the law requires.

We'll notify you by email at the address on your account, and in the app if we can.

16. Changes to this policy

We'll update this policy when the app changes or the law does.

  • Minor changes (typos, clarifications): we update the "last updated" date at the top.
  • Meaningful changes — new information collected, a new purpose, a new service provider, a change to what other users can see: we'll tell you in the app and by email at least 30 days before the change takes effect, and give you a chance to review it or delete your account first.

We won't quietly start using your information for something new. If a change means collecting or using your information for a purpose you didn't agree to, we'll ask for your consent again.
